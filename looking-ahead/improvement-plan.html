Student participation in lecture

The observers' requests. Last fall's observation reports ask for more structured interaction between students during lecture. The specifics were wait time after a question, repeating student answers aloud and using names, Think-Pair-Share, and sharing student work on the document camera. The reports themselves are on the Committee Observation Feedback page.

Status of each request. Wait time after a question is part of how I run lecture now. Peer explanation is built into this fall's CHEM V101 lectures as a short peer teach-back: while working a problem, students explain a step aloud to a partner, in the chapters where explaining carries the concept. I designed it over the summer as part of the Math Readiness work, for the fluent student who learns most by explaining, and it is the kind of peer interaction the observers had asked for. In practice it has settled at the moment a practice block opens, and it has yet to show up inside an explanation. This fall's feedback reminded me to repeat student answers aloud. I went back through the transcripts of this fall's lectures to see how often I was doing it, and found it in every recorded lecture in both sections. The feedback had caught something narrower, which is that a quiet answer can reach me and still be lost to the rest of the room, and I now make sure those are heard. The same transcripts show that I rarely use students' names, and that is the part I have yet to make a habit. For students who would rather not answer in front of the room, I tell them they can signal me with a look, the way people bid at an auction, and I read those signals as I go.

The document camera. Sharing student work on the document camera was a colleague's suggestion after an observation in Fall 2025. I tried it in lecture that October, with two correct solutions and one incorrect one side by side, and the observer that day found that the comparison made the concept clearer. It also put one student's error in front of the whole room, which felt closer to public embarrassment than to feedback, and it interrupted the way I normally run a problem session. I have stopped showing an individual student's work. I already know which mistakes come up most often, so I point those out during lecture, and no one in the room has to be the example. In the peer teach-back a student's error is heard by one partner.

My colleagues' suggestions have given me most of what I have tried in lecture, and I keep learning from how they teach. Alongside that, I want a higher-resolution picture of how I teach best, so after each class I note how the things I tried went, and I use those notes to decide what to keep and how to adjust it. This fall the committee and I have talked about fitting these practices to the way I teach.
